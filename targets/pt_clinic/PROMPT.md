# Lantern Row Physical Therapy: an outpatient practice paid mostly by insurers

This describes one business, its past and its year. Facts marked † come from the real practice's public Regulation Crowdfunding filing, or from the payers' own published rules (see `SOURCES.md`); a † vouches for the fact it follows, not for the date or reason the story gives it. The practice, its people and its account numbers are invented.

## 1. The business and the time frame

- **Business:** Lantern Row Physical Therapy PLLC, an outpatient physical therapy practice on Capitol Hill in Washington, DC, with one 1,200 sq ft clinic†. Its specialty is joint replacement: it sees patients before surgery, on the day of surgery at two surgery centers it has contracts with, at home in the weeks after, and then in the clinic†. It is employee-owned†.
- **Size:** about 28 people† by late 2023: the founder, a director of outpatient rehab, an office manager, a billing manager, three patient care coordinators, about seven full-time therapists, a contract therapist, a telehealth therapist, about ten per-visit (PRN) therapists and a part-time aide†. An outside CPA does the books†.
- **Volume:** about 60 patient visits a day† at an average of about `$125` collected per visit†, or about `$150,000` a month†. Revenue was about `$510,000` in 2021†, `$1,035,000` in 2022† and about `$1,535,000` in 2023†. The practice first turns a profit in the fourth quarter of 2023†.
- **Time range:** 2023-01-01 to 2023-12-31, currency USD.
- **Who pays:** most of each visit is paid by insurers weeks after the visit: Medicare, the region's dominant Blue Cross plan and other commercial insurers, DC Medicaid managed-care plans, workers' compensation and auto insurers. Patients pay copays, deductibles and coinsurance at the front desk or later by card. The surgery centers pay a monthly contract fee. A few patients pay cash for services insurance does not cover, such as dry needling.

## 2. History before the start

- The founder, a doctor of physical therapy with 13 years in practice, most recently managing clinics for private-equity-owned chains†, incorporated in July 2020†. The first surgery-center contract was signed in August 2020 and the second in November†; the first patient was seen in October 2020†, before there was a clinic, in surgery centers and patients' homes. The first full-time therapist and a part-time office manager were hired in December 2020†.
- The clinic opened in mid-2021 on a 6-year lease†. Equipment and the build-out were paid for partly with a loan from a community development lender (WACIF)† that is repaid monthly.
- Medicare pays the practice through its regional contractor, and every payment comes with a 2% sequestration reduction. Clean electronic claims cannot be paid before the 14th day after they are received†.
- On 2023-01-01: about four weeks of visits are billed and unpaid across all payers; Medicare claims for the last two weeks of December are inside their 14-day wait; a stack of commercial claims for a therapist hired in November is on hold until that payer finishes credentialing him; patient deductibles have just reset, so January's visits will cost patients far more out of pocket; December's surgery-center invoices are outstanding; the line of credit is mostly drawn.

## 3. Institutions (the only sources of data)

- **Bank (Capital Row Bank):** operating checking (`••5520`) and a line of credit (`••5521`). Every insurer payment lands here as an ACH credit whose description carries the payer's name and a trace number, never the patient. Paper checks from small payers arrive by mail and are deposited in batches.
- **Clearinghouse and practice software:** claims go out electronically each evening; electronic remittances come back listing, for each claim, what was billed, allowed, paid, adjusted and left to the patient, with standard reason codes. The software also records front-desk charges and patient balances.
- **Card processor:** front-desk and online card payments for copays and balances, paid out to checking the next business day net of fees; refunds go back to the card.
- **Payroll provider:** payroll every two weeks for staff therapists and front-desk staff; PRN therapists are paid per visit through the same runs. The contract therapist invoices monthly.
- **Lenders:** the WACIF loan (monthly ACH)†, the bank line of credit (monthly interest; draws and paydowns)†, and short-term financing† that takes the form of a merchant cash advance repaid by daily debits.

## 4. The cast

- **Dr. Owen Reyes, founder and president.** A clinician first, a businessman second. Sees patients three days a week, signs the payroll, and decides which bill waits when the insurers are slow. Certified in dry needling and offers it as a cash service.
- **Priya Raman, director of outpatient rehab.** Runs the schedule and the therapists, and hires; hiring licensed therapists is the hardest part of the job†.
- **Gail Watters, billing manager.** The person who knows where every dollar is. Works denials, appeals, credentialing and payer portals; refuses virtual-card payments when she can.
- **Monique Abara, office manager.** Pays vendors, manages the lease, the card terminal and supplies.
- **Patient care coordinators (three).** Check patients in, verify insurance, collect copays, set up payment plans.
- **Therapists:** salaried staff, PRN therapists who pick up visits and are paid per visit, one telehealth therapist, one contract therapist billing monthly.
- **Patients:** joint-replacement patients (mostly on Medicare, many with a supplemental plan that pays the 20% Medicare leaves), commercial patients with copays of `$20-60`, high-deductible patients who owe the full allowed amount early in the year, workers' compensation and auto-accident patients, and a few cash patients.
- **Payers:** Medicare's regional contractor; the dominant regional Blue Cross plan; two national commercial insurers; two DC Medicaid managed-care plans; a workers' compensation carrier; an auto insurer.
- **Surgery centers (two):** contract customers, invoiced monthly for pre-op and day-of-surgery therapy, paying by ACH on net-30.
- **Vendors:** the landlord (rent by ACH), the practice software and clearinghouse subscription, medical supply and equipment suppliers, malpractice and business insurance, the outside CPA, a staffing agency used once, and a parking garage for home-visit cars.

## 5. Money flows

| Flow | How it moves |
|---|---|
| Visit -> claim -> payer | claims sent nightly; payment by ACH with an electronic remittance, weeks later, for less than billed |
| Medicare -> bank | ACH no earlier than 14 days after an electronic claim is received†, reduced 2%; patient's 20% crosses over to the supplemental plan automatically |
| Commercial payers -> bank | ACH in 2-6 weeks, some by virtual card (charged through the card processor with a fee) or paper check |
| Payer -> practice, taking back | an overpayment found later is subtracted from a future payment, so an ACH arrives smaller than its claims |
| Patients -> card processor -> bank | copays at check-in, balances after the remittance, payment plans charged monthly; next-day payouts net of fees |
| Practice -> patients | refunds of overpaid balances to the card or by check |
| Surgery centers -> bank | monthly contract invoices on net-30 |
| Payroll provider -> staff | every two weeks; PRN pay varies with visits |
| Practice -> contract therapist, landlord, vendors | monthly ACH and card |
| Practice -> WACIF, bank, cash-advance company | monthly loan payment, monthly line-of-credit interest, daily advance debits |

## 6. Stories

- **January's deductible reset.** Patients' deductibles start over on January 1. Remittances in January and February leave much more to patients; coordinators collect at the desk, set up payment plans, and some balances go unpaid for months. Cash dips just as Medicare's fee cut for 2023 shows in every payment (declining Medicare reimbursement is a challenge the real practice names†).
- **The new therapist who can't be billed yet (January-March).** The therapist hired in November is credentialed with Medicare quickly but with the Blue Cross plan only in March. His claims to that plan are held, then sent all at once, and a burst of payments lands in April.
- **The rate cut at renewal (spring).** The Blue Cross contract renews with lower rates for the most common codes (downward pressure on in-network rates is the other challenge the real practice names†). Each payment is a little smaller than the same visit paid last year.
- **A cash advance, then another (April and August).** With payroll due and insurers slow, Dr. Reyes takes a merchant cash advance repaid by a fixed daily debit; in August he takes a second one from another company on top of the first. By December debt totals about `$300,000`†, across the WACIF loan, the line of credit and the short-term advances†, against about `$40,000` in cash†. The plan for early 2024 is to raise money from customers and neighbors through a revenue-sharing note to open a second clinic and pay off the short-term debt†.
- **The recoupment (June).** A commercial payer audits a batch of claims, decides it overpaid, and takes the money back by shrinking two later payments. Gail appeals; half is returned in October.
- **Virtual cards (summer).** One payer starts paying by virtual card; each card is charged through the card processor at a 3% fee. Gail opts the practice out, and after two more card payments the payer switches back to ACH.
- **Denials and appeals.** A run of claims is denied for a missing referral; Gail resubmits them with the referral and they are paid six weeks late. A workers' compensation claim takes four months.
- **Hiring for growth (fall).** Two therapists sign for January and February 2024 starts† and the 30th employee signs in December†. Payroll grows; the practice is profitable for the quarter†.
- **Surgery center pays late.** One surgery center misses two monthly invoices during an ownership change and then pays both with one ACH.

## 7. Kinds of events

Over the year, its records show these kinds of events:

- **Insurers:** claims paid weeks after the visit for less than billed; Medicare's 14-day wait and 2% reduction; supplemental plans paying the patient's 20%; denials and resubmissions; an appeal partly won; a recoupment netted out of later payments; virtual-card payments with fees; paper checks deposited in batches; claims held for credentialing and then paid in a burst; a rate cut at contract renewal.
- **Patients:** copays at check-in; deductible-heavy balances early in the year; payment plans; unpaid balances; refunds of overpayments; cash-pay services.
- **Contracts:** monthly surgery-center invoices, one paid late in a lump.
- **People:** biweekly payroll with per-visit pay for PRN therapists; a contract therapist's monthly invoice; new hires.
- **Financing:** a monthly community-lender loan payment, line-of-credit interest and draws, merchant cash advances with daily debits, two stacked.
- **Institutions:** next-day card payouts net of fees; insurer ACH credits that name the payer, never the patient; monthly statements.
