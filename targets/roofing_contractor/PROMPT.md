# Kestrel Roofing Company: a union commercial roofer sold to a new owner mid-year

This describes one business, its past and its year. Facts marked † come from the real company's public Regulation Crowdfunding filing that this business is modeled on (see `SOURCES.md`); a † vouches for the fact it follows, not for the date or reason the story gives it. The company, its people and its account numbers are invented.

## 1. The business and the time frame

- **Business:** Kestrel Roofing Company, Inc., a commercial roofing contractor in Rockford, Illinois, incorporated in 1998† and serving northern Illinois and southern Wisconsin†. It replaces and repairs flat and low-slope roofs on warehouses, factories, schools, government buildings and retail centers†, installs roofs on new buildings for general contractors and developers†, runs inspection and maintenance programs, and answers emergency leak calls around the clock†. It installs PVC, TPO, EPDM and modified-bitumen membranes and retrofits metal roofs†, and as a certified installer for a PVC manufacturer it can offer the owner a No Dollar Limit warranty†.
- **Labor:** a union contractor† whose roofers belong to the regional roofers' local. Crews are paid weekly at union scale; the company sends fringe-benefit contributions for every hour worked to the union's trust funds each month.
- **Size:** revenue was `$5,147,755` in 2023† and `$4,423,556` in 2024†. Operating expenses rose from about `$1,066,000` to about `$1,679,000`† as general and administrative costs grew and marketing rose by about `$100,000`†.
- **Time range:** 2024-01-01 to 2024-12-31, currency USD. The roofing season runs April to November; winter is repairs, emergencies and bidding.

## 2. History before the start

- A family founded the company in 1998 and ran it for 26 years. It has steady relationships with a few general contractors, several school districts and many building owners who call it when a roof leaks.
- On 2024-01-01 it has about `$1,834,000` in cash† and no bank debt beyond truck loans. Dozens of jobs from the fall are invoiced and unpaid, including retainage held on two new buildings and a school job waiting for its punch list. Most roofers are laid off for the winter and collecting unemployment; a skeleton crew handles service calls.
- Over the winter the founding family agrees to sell the company to an outside buyer, an entrepreneur who owns a holding company with other small businesses. The sale is set to close on May 1, 2024.

## 3. Institutions (the only sources of data)

- **Rock River Bank:** the operating checking account (`••3381`) and a payroll account (`••3382`). Posted transactions with running balances and monthly statements. Owners' checks and school-district warrants arrive by mail and are deposited in batches; some customers pay by ACH.
- **Pathward Bank:** after the sale, a 10-year term loan at 9.75% maturing May 2034†, repaid in monthly installments (about `$3.7 million` was still owed when the company filed in 2025†), and a revolving line of credit at 9.75% maturing May 2026† (about `$428,000` drawn at that filing†). Monthly loan statements.
- **Ford Motor Credit:** loans on the company's pickup and box trucks at 5.99%† (about `$86,000` outstanding at the 2025 filing†), monthly ACH.
- **Construction payroll provider:** weekly payroll for crews and office staff; certified payroll reports for public jobs that require prevailing wages; union dues deducted from paychecks.
- **Union trust funds:** a monthly remittance report and payment for health and welfare, pension, annuity and training contributions on every hour worked, due by the 15th of the following month.
- **Business credit card and fuel cards:** materials picked up at the counter, fuel, hotel nights on Wisconsin jobs, small tools; monthly statement paid from checking.
- **Surety:** performance and payment bonds for public jobs, each with a premium.

## 4. The cast

- **Walt Halvorsen, the seller.** Second-generation owner, 64, who knows every school facilities director in three counties. After the sale he holds a `$457,000` note from the company at 6%† and stays on for six months as a paid consultant.
- **Derek Saunders, the buyer and new owner.** Energetic and ambitious; wants to triple the company. Brings a marketing contractor, a new website, a CRM, branded trucks and an aggressive hiring plan. Uses Kestrel's cash to fund his holding company, recorded as loans to it.
- **Linda Ostrowski, office manager and bookkeeper.** Twenty-two years at the company. Runs pay applications, lien waivers, certified payroll, union reports and receivables; records each transfer to the holding company carefully as an amount it owes Kestrel.
- **Ray Delgado, operations manager and estimator.** Bids jobs, orders materials, schedules crews, and picks up materials on the company card.
- **Foremen and crews.** Four crews of 4-6 union roofers each in season, called back in April and laid off in late November.
- **Customers:**
  - Building owners and property managers who pay a deposit, a progress payment and the balance at completion, by check or ACH, some slowly.
  - General contractors who pay monthly applications for payment less 10% retainage, only after they are paid by the owner, and only against signed lien waivers. Retainage is released at completion, months later.
  - School districts and municipalities: public bids, prevailing wages, bonds, payment by warrant after each monthly board meeting, and retainage released after final acceptance.
  - Owners with storm damage whose insurers pay in two checks: the actual cash value first, and the withheld depreciation after the work is complete, sometimes made out jointly to the owner and the mortgage lender.
  - Maintenance-program customers billed quarterly.
- **Vendors:** two roofing distributors on net-30 terms with a 2% discount for paying by the 10th of the next month; the membrane manufacturer, paid directly for fabricated membranes and warranty fees on each job; equipment rental (cranes, boom lifts); dumpster haulers; the insurance agency (general liability, auto, workers' compensation with a year-end premium audit); the surety; the marketing contractor and software subscriptions after the sale.

## 6. Stories

- **The sale closes (May 1).** Pathward funds the term loan into Kestrel's account† and the money moves on the same day to the buyer's holding company, which pays the family; Kestrel signs the `$457,000` note to Walt†. From then on, Kestrel pays the term loan every month. Over the rest of the year Kestrel advances more cash to the holding company; by December the holding company owes Kestrel about `$4.16 million`†, and Kestrel's cash has fallen to about `$599,000`†.
- **A slow spring.** With the sale taking the owners' attention, Kestrel loses two bids it usually wins and starts the season late; revenue for the year ends about 14% below 2023†.
- **The new owner spends on growth (May-December).** A marketing contractor on a monthly retainer, a new website, wrapped trucks financed through Ford Motor Credit, a CRM subscription, and two new office hires. Marketing and administrative costs climb†.
- **The school job (June-November).** A district's high-school roof replacement, bid at prevailing wages with a performance and payment bond. Linda files certified payroll every week. The district pays a monthly warrant after each board meeting, less retainage; one board meeting is cancelled in July, so two months arrive together in August. Retainage is released in November after the punch list.
- **The warehouse for a general contractor (July-December).** Monthly pay applications less 10% retainage. The developer pays the general contractor late in September, so Kestrel waits too; the general contractor's payment arrives with a request for an unconditional lien waiver. Retainage is still held at year end.
- **The hailstorm (June 18).** Emergency calls for two days, then several insurance-claim replacements. Insurers pay the first checks in July; the depreciation checks come in the fall after completion photos, one of them a joint check that needs the mortgage lender's endorsement and takes three weeks.
- **Cash gets tight (fall).** Kestrel draws on the Pathward line to buy materials in September and misses the distributors' early-payment discount for two months. One union remittance goes in on the 22nd instead of the 15th, and the trust fund bills a late charge.
- **Winter (late November-December).** Crews are laid off; service calls for snow-load leaks; the workers' compensation premium audit adds an extra premium; the year-end bonus run for the office.

## 7. Kinds of events

Over the year, its records show these kinds of events:

- **Customers:** deposits and progress payments, monthly pay applications less retainage, pay-when-paid delays, retainage released months after completion, retainage still held at year end, public-entity warrants after board meetings, insurer payments in two parts, a joint check, quarterly maintenance billing.
- **Ownership change:** a term loan funded at closing, a seller note, transfers to a related company that build up a large receivable, a new owner's spending on marketing and staff.
- **Labor:** weekly union payroll, seasonal recall and layoff, certified payroll on public jobs, monthly union trust fund remittances, one late with a charge, union dues deducted.
- **Purchases:** distributor accounts with early-payment discounts taken and missed, manufacturer purchases and warranty fees, equipment rental, dumpsters, card purchases at the counter, fuel.
- **Financing and insurance:** monthly term-loan installments, line-of-credit draws and interest, truck loans, bond premiums, a premium audit.
- **Institutions:** checks and warrants deposited in batches, ACH payments, monthly statements, a payroll account funded from operating.
