# Triangle Shield Software: a tiny listed data-security company closing its books on prepaid multi-year subscriptions (60 days, summer 2023)

This describes one business, its past and sixty days. Facts marked † come from the real company's public filings that this business is modeled on: its 10-K, an amended 10-K, and the quarterly balances it tagged in its filings (see `SOURCES.md`). A mark vouches for the fact it follows, not for the date or reason the story gives it. The company, its people and its account numbers are invented.

This world is built for testing record-to-report agents: the people who close the month and the quarter. The money is small and the work is in the schedules:

- unearned revenue from subscriptions paid years in advance;
- prepaid costs that have to be spread over time;
- payroll that straddles month ends;
- a stack of convertible notes that change every time a lender converts;
- a statement of cash flows that turns out to be wrong.

## 1. The business and the time frame

- **Business:** Triangle Shield Software, Inc., based in Research Triangle Park, North Carolina†. It sells data security and privacy software†:
  - **VaultRoom:** secure sharing of sensitive content.
  - **ArchiveCrate:** email and data archiving.
  - **DataCourier:** data placement.
  - **TagSense:** data classification.
  - **RewindGuard:** ransomware recovery, bought in January 2022.
  - **PrivacyKit:** privacy plugins for WordPress sites (GDPR, CCPA and LGPD), some of them free with paid upgrades†.
- **How it sells:** Mostly subscriptions, often paid in advance for several years and renewing automatically†. Revenue is recognized evenly over the subscription term†. Older customers bought perpetual licenses with yearly maintenance, and the company is moving them to subscriptions†. It sells directly and through channel partners, distributors and resellers†, and through the Microsoft Azure and AWS marketplaces†. It counts more than 10,000 customers†, most of them small plugin buyers.
- **Size:**
  - **Revenue.** `$3,609,494` in 2021 and `$2,627,123` in 2022†. `$1,379,806` in the first quarter of 2023, `$619,040` in the second† and `$693,835` in the third†.
  - **Unearned revenue (deferred revenue).** `$1,584,809` on March 31, 2023, `$1,814,620` on June 30† and `$1,619,711` on September 30†.
  - **Accrued liabilities.** `$553,367` on March 31 and `$778,611` on June 30†.
  - **Results.** The 2022 net loss was `$9,713,467`†, mostly interest and debt discounts.
  - **Cash.** The company ended 2022 with `$1,712` in cash†.
- **Staff:** 22 employees and 5 independent contractors†. Finance is a CFO, a staff accountant and an outside bookkeeper.
- **Time range:** 2023-07-01 to 2023-08-29, sixty days, currency USD. The window covers:
  - the June close and the second-quarter report;
  - the July close;
  - the start of the August close;
  - the Fourth of July (a Tuesday);
  - semi-monthly paydays on July 14, July 31 and August 15, and the August 31 payroll processed on August 29;
  - the quarterly report filed on August 14†;
  - an amended annual report filed on August 24†.

## 2. History before the start

- **Acquisitions.** The company has grown by buying small software products and their customers. In January 2022 it bought the ransomware recovery product for `$3.4 million`: `$250,000` in cash, a `$2.9 million` promissory note and a `$250,000` contingent payment†. It moved to a five-year office lease, signed by its North Carolina subsidiary in July 2022†.
- **Debt.** It is financed by a stack of short-term convertible notes from small-cap lenders, ordinary promissory notes and loans from its CEO†. Its obligations are secured by all of its assets†. In 2022 it raised `$2,027,570` of convertible debt and `$3,458,247` of notes, repaid `$771,718` of convertible notes and `$4,408,240` of notes†, and borrowed `$299,280` from the CEO, who also paid `$167,653` of company expenses himself†. Most notes can be converted into shares at a discount to the market price, and lenders convert in pieces.
- **Controls.** The last annual report named four weaknesses in internal control†:
  - "We did not have controls designed to validate the completeness and accuracy of underlying data."
  - "We do not have written documentation of our internal control policies and procedures."
  - "We do not have sufficient segregation of duties within accounting functions."
  - "We do not have a functioning audit committee or outside directors on our board of directors."
- **On 2023-07-01:**
  - June's books are open. The June bank reconciliation is not done.
  - The deferred revenue schedule was last rolled forward at March 31.
  - The prepaid schedule has three new items from the spring that nobody has set up.
  - June 16-30 payroll was paid on June 30, but commissions earned in June are not accrued.
  - Two marketplace disbursements for May and June are unbooked.
  - Five convertible notes are outstanding, and one lender sent a conversion notice on June 29.
  - The directors-and-officers insurance renews on July 1.

## 3. Institutions (the only sources of data)

Each keeps its own records the way it really would. Contracts, invoices, statements, conversion notices and reports are records too: one row per document, with the fields printed on it.

- **Piedmont Ridge Bank:** operating account `••5180`. Each posted transaction with the bank's description, a running balance that often dips below zero for a day, and monthly statements.
- **QuickBooks Online (the books):**
  - the chart of accounts, invoices, bills, payments, deposits, journal entries and the trial balance;
  - a single deferred revenue account;
  - a prepaid expenses account;
  - one liability account per note, with discount accounts beside them.

  Journal entries record who posted them and when. The CFO can post, edit and delete any entry, including in closed months, because nobody has locked them.
- **The close spreadsheets (kept by the CFO on a shared drive):**
  - the deferred revenue schedule, contract by contract, with start and end dates;
  - the prepaid schedule;
  - the convertible note schedule, with principal, discount, accrued interest and conversions;
  - the payroll accrual;
  - the cash flow worksheet that builds the statement of cash flows.

  Each version is a file with a date in its name.
- **The CRM:** every customer contract with its products, term, price, billing schedule and renewal date. Sales enters it, sometimes after the invoice.
- **AWS Marketplace and Microsoft's commercial marketplace:** each has buyer subscriptions, a monthly billing report and disbursements to the company's bank account after the marketplace has collected from the buyer, net of its fee.
- **The plugin store:** sells PrivacyKit licenses by card, yearly and lifetime. It keeps refunds and chargebacks and pays out monthly, net of its revenue share, with a sales report behind each payout.
- **Resellers and distributors:** buy multi-year subscriptions on purchase orders, pay invoices on net 30 to net 60, and send remittance advice by email.
- **The payroll provider:** semi-monthly payroll for 22 employees. The pay period ends five days before payday, so pay is always a few days behind the work. It keeps a register per run and debits the bank two days before payday. Contractors invoice monthly.
- **The insurance premium finance company:** pays the D&O premium to the insurer up front and collects nine monthly installments from the bank.
- **Lenders and the transfer agent:** lenders send conversion notices by email (principal and interest converted, conversion price, number of shares). The transfer agent issues the shares and keeps the share register.
- **The SEC's EDGAR system:** the company's filings, with their filing dates.

## 4. The cast

- **Jordan Reese, founder and CEO.** Sells the big deals. Lends the company money when payroll is short, and pays some bills on his own card. Signs every filing.
- **Priya Natarajan, CFO (part-time, three days a week).** Owns the close, the schedules and the filings. Posts most journal entries herself, often on Sunday evenings. Nobody reviews her entries.
- **Ben Carter, staff accountant.** Matches the bank feed in QuickBooks, enters bills and books payouts. Started in May.
- **Outside bookkeeper.** Processes payroll and contractor invoices.
- **Sales team (6).** Close multi-year deals near quarter end and sometimes promise free months that never reach the CRM.
- **Customers:**
  - banks, sports teams and public bodies on multi-year prepaid subscriptions;
  - resellers;
  - marketplace buyers;
  - thousands of website owners buying plugin licenses.
- **Lenders.** Five noteholders with conversion rights, plus the seller of the ransomware product, owed the promissory note.

## 5. Money flows

| Flow | How it moves |
|---|---|
| Direct customers and resellers -> operating account | ACH, wire and check against invoices, many of them multi-year prepayments |
| Marketplace buyers -> AWS / Microsoft -> operating account | monthly disbursements, net of the marketplace fee, after the buyer pays |
| Plugin buyers -> plugin store -> operating account | monthly payout, net of revenue share, refunds and chargebacks |
| Operating account -> payroll provider -> employees, tax agencies | semi-monthly, two days before payday |
| Operating account -> contractors, AWS hosting, software, rent | monthly |
| Operating account -> premium finance company | nine monthly installments from August |
| Operating account -> noteholders | scheduled repayments on some notes |
| Noteholders -> shares | conversions of principal and interest, no cash |
| CEO -> operating account, CEO's card -> vendors | loans when cash is short, company bills paid personally |

## 6. Stories

- **The June close (July 1-14).** Priya closes June two weeks late. The deferred revenue schedule has to be rolled forward three months, from March 31 to June 30. New multi-year deals signed in the quarter are billed in full on signing, but two of them are in the CRM with the wrong start dates. One reseller deal gives the customer three free months at the start that nobody entered. The schedule's total at June 30 must come to `$1,814,620`†.
- **The prepaid pile (July).** Three spring purchases were booked straight to expense: a year of a sales software subscription, a prepaid trade-show booth for October and a committed hosting plan. The D&O policy renews on July 1 for a year. It is financed through the premium finance company, which pays the insurer and starts debiting installments in August. Ben books the first installment as insurance expense, not as a payment against the finance company's loan.
- **The payroll straddle (all window).** Each payday pays a period that ended five days earlier. At every month end, wages for the last days of the month are unpaid and must be accrued. June commissions on the big quarter-end deals are paid with the July 31 payroll and belong in June.
- **Marketplace payouts (July-August).** AWS disburses May and June together in July. Microsoft's payout for June includes a refund for a customer who cancelled in May. Ben books each disbursement as revenue for the month it arrives, not as collection of the marketplace receivable for months already earned.
- **Conversions (all window).** Noteholders convert seven times in the window, each time a slice of principal and accrued interest at a discount to the share price. Each conversion reduces the note, writes off part of its unamortized discount and issues shares. Priya books them from the conversion notices. One notice converts interest the schedule had not accrued yet.
- **The CEO's money (July).** Payroll on July 14 is `$41,000` short. Jordan lends `$45,000` on July 13 and pays a `$6,200` software renewal on his own card. Both belong in the loans-from-related-parties account. The card payment reaches the books only when he emails the receipt in August.
- **The second-quarter report (August 1-14).** Priya prepares the quarterly report and its statement of cash flows. She files it on August 14†.
- **The cash flow mistake (August).** While preparing the second-quarter cash flows, Priya finds that the 2022 annual statement of cash flows was wrong. It showed certain convertible-note balances as financing cash flows when they belonged in operating†. The amended annual report, filed on August 24†, changes cash used in operations for 2022 from `$2,886,337` to `$1,252,650` and financing cash from `$2,244,244` to `$610,557`†. The same worksheet error is still in this year's quarterly worksheets.
- **July close (August).** Priya closes July on August 20-27. She posts the deferred revenue release, the prepaid amortization, the payroll accrual, interest and discount amortization on the notes, and a round `$25,000` "true-up" to deferred revenue to make the schedule match the trial balance. She posts all of it on a Sunday, with no support attached.

## 7. Kinds of events

Over the sixty days, its records show these kinds of events:

- **Revenue and unearned revenue:** multi-year subscriptions billed up front, revenue released evenly each month, wrong start dates, free months, renewals, a cancellation and refund, marketplace and plugin-store sales, reseller deals.
- **Prepaids:** annual subscriptions and deposits booked to expense instead of prepaid, an insurance premium financed and paid in installments, monthly amortization.
- **Payroll and accruals:** semi-monthly payroll with periods that straddle month ends, wage and commission accruals, contractor invoices that arrive late.
- **Debt and equity:** note conversions into shares, discount amortization, accrued interest, scheduled repayments, loans from the CEO and bills he paid himself.
- **Cash:** bank feed matching, marketplace and plugin payouts booked as summary entries, a balance that goes negative for a day.
- **Close and reporting:** month-end closes run late, journal entries posted on weekends by one person with no review, a round-dollar adjustment with no support, a quarterly report, an amended annual report fixing the cash flow statement.

## 8. What the world must also remember

So that an agent working these records can be graded, the world keeps, in its own state and not in any institution's records, the truth behind every record:

- the correct deferred revenue for each contract at every month end, and the right split between amounts due within a year and later;
- the correct prepaid balances and monthly amortization;
- the payroll and commission accruals each month should carry;
- each note's correct principal, discount, interest and conversions;
- which cash movements belong in operating, investing and financing;
- what the round-dollar adjustment was hiding;
- the trial balance as it should be at June 30 and July 31.
