# Kindred Cotton Co.: a direct-to-consumer brand that lands one huge institutional customer

This describes one business, its past and its year. Facts marked † come from the real company's public Regulation Crowdfunding filing that this business is modeled on (see `SOURCES.md`); a † vouches for the fact it follows, not for the date or reason the story gives it. The company, its people and its account numbers are invented.

## 1. The business and the time frame

- **Business:** Kindred Cotton Co., a Los Angeles brand of organic, plastic-free period products: tampons, pads, liners, cups and period underwear†, shipped in compostable packaging. It sells on its own Shopify store, wholesale to natural-food stores and co-ops, and in 2022 to large institutional buyers†.
- **Size:** 3 employees† (the two founders and one warehouse lead). Most of the work is done by contractors†: a fractional bookkeeper, a marketing agency, a designer, a customer-service contractor and a part-time packer. It rents a small warehouse in LA† for about `$4,150` a month (`$49,750` in 2022†).
- **Time range:** 2022-01-01 to 2022-12-31, currency USD.
- **Revenue history†:** `$987,796` in 2020 (a pandemic spike), `$574,528` in 2021, `$1,145,548` in 2022.
- **2022 by channel†:** direct-to-consumer `$262,550`, wholesale `$35,673`, institutional (B2B) `$849,023`, returns `$1,250`. Little sales tax is collected (`$447` remitted†).
- **2022 costs (for scale)†:** product `$427,399` plus inbound freight `$21,943`; contractors `$140,743`; marketing `$54,665`; software `$30,705`; postage and outbound shipping `$21,751`; merchant fees `$13,558`; interest `$10,854`; payments on a marketing advance `$11,445`.
- **The shape of the year:** profitable on paper (`$330,228` net income†) and nearly out of cash, because almost all of the institutional revenue is still unpaid at year end: receivables of `$965,325`† against `$41,395` in the bank†.

## 2. History before the start

- The founders have worked together for 16 years† and started the business on a kitchen counter, beginning operations in January 2017†. The company is women-, LGBTQIA+- and Latina-owned†.
- Early money came from convertible notes (2017-2019, at 2-4%)†, a `$100,000` promissory note at 5%†, a Regulation Crowdfunding convertible note round (2018)† and a crowdfunding SAFE round (2021, about `$105,000`)†. None of these require payments yet.
- The 2020 pandemic spike filled the order book; 2021 fell back and lost money†.
- The bank is a Citi business checking account† (`Checking ••2218`) and the company card is Brex†. Through 2021 the bookkeeper kept the books on a cash basis; from 2022 they move to accrual†.
- On 2022-01-01: about `$140,000` in checking†; a few hundred subscribers on monthly plans with cards stored in Shopify; two co-ops owe for December orders; the Brex December statement awaits its autopay; a container of underwear from the contract manufacturer is at sea with half its price still owed.

## 3. Institutions (the only sources of data)

- **Citi:** business checking. Each posted transaction with a running balance, and monthly statements. Wires out to the manufacturer carry a fee.
- **Shopify (store and Shopify Payments):** orders, subscription renewals, refunds, chargebacks and payouts to checking every business day, net of fees. Shopify Capital advances† are repaid by holding back a fixed share of each day's sales from the payout.
- **Brex:** the company card for ads, software, shipping labels and supplies, with a monthly statement paid in full from checking.
- **Revenue-based lenders:** Clearco† (a marketing advance repaid as a share of revenue each week), Wayflyer† (an advance repaid as a share of daily revenue), and Fundbox† (a line drawn in chunks, each repaid in fixed weekly debits over 12 weeks). A small bank loan from a community bank (CBB)† is repaid monthly.
- **Shipping platform:** buys USPS and UPS labels, billed to the Brex card in batches; adjustments come in weeks later when a carrier re-measures a box.

## 4. The cast

- **Inés Navarro, co-founder and CEO.** Sells. Spent 2021 chasing institutional buyers and wins in 2022. Generous, fast-moving, accepts financing offers in the Shopify dashboard on her phone.
- **Robin Hale, co-founder and COO.** Runs product, the manufacturer and the warehouse; watches cash in a spreadsheet and worries about every advance Inés accepts.
- **Tomás Reyes, warehouse lead.** Picks, packs and ships; receives containers; counts inventory badly in the busy months.
- **Contractors:** a fractional bookkeeper paid monthly, a marketing agency on a monthly retainer plus ad spend on Brex, a designer billed by project, a customer-service contractor, a part-time packer paid every two weeks by ACH.
- **Direct customers:** a few hundred monthly subscribers who renew on a set day (some cards decline and are retried; some cancel, some pause), plus one-time buyers who spike around promotions. A few dispute a charge each month, mostly "item not received".
- **Wholesale customers:** a dozen natural-food stores and co-ops on net-30, paying by check or ACH, a few always late.
- **Institutional customer:** the purchasing cooperative for a group of school districts, which must stock free period products in school restrooms starting with the new school year. It buys in large purchase orders on net-60 terms and pays through a slow procurement office. (Which institution it is, is this world's choice; the filing shows only the B2B revenue and the receivable.)
- **Vendors:** the contract manufacturer abroad (wire 30% at order and 70% before shipment), a freight forwarder and customs broker (duties and freight by ACH), the warehouse landlord (ACH on the 1st), Shopify and app subscriptions, the shipping platform, a packaging supplier for compostable mailers.

## 5. Money flows

| Flow | How it moves |
|---|---|
| Direct customers -> Shopify -> Citi | daily payouts net of fees and refunds; chargebacks pulled back with a fee |
| Shopify Capital -> Citi | an advance deposited once; then a fixed share of each day's sales held back from payouts until repaid |

| Company -> Brex | monthly statement autopay |
| Company -> contractors, landlord | monthly ACH; the packer every two weeks |
| Company -> CBB | monthly loan payment |

## 6. Stories

- **The big win (March-May).** After months of sales calls, the school purchasing cooperative names Kindred an approved vendor. The first purchase order arrives in May for delivery in August, before school starts. Inés celebrates; Robin calculates that the order needs more product than the warehouse holds.
- **Stacking advances to fund inventory (May-August).** To pay the manufacturer's deposit and balance, Inés accepts a Shopify Capital advance†, draws twice on Fundbox†, and takes a Wayflyer advance†; Clearco funds a summer ad push†. By August, several lenders are taking a share of the same daily sales, and the Shopify payouts shrink sharply. In 2022 the company borrows about `$164,000`† and repays about `$52,000`† of it during the year, finishing with every advance paid off†.
- **Shipping to the schools (August-October).** Pallets go out by freight in three waves. The cooperative's receiving office rejects one shipment's paperwork, so that invoice is not approved for payment; a second invoice is short-paid over cases damaged in transit; a third is paid on day 75.
- **Year end with almost nothing in the bank (November-December).** Receivables reach about `$965,000`†, nearly all of it from the cooperative, while checking falls to about `$41,000`†. Robin delays a contractor invoice and the packaging order; Inés asks the cooperative for a payment status every week. The cooperative promises a large payment for the first week of January, after this world ends.
- **Subscribers churn and come back.** A price increase in April adds a dollar to each plan; a dozen subscribers cancel, several pause for the summer and resume in September, and a holiday promotion brings in a spike of one-time orders and a cluster of "item not received" disputes in January.
- **The carrier adjustments.** The shipping platform bills label adjustments weeks after the shipments when the carrier re-measures boxes, in small amounts added to Brex.
- **Giving product away.** The founders donate cases to a local shelter and give `$1,760` in cash to community groups through the year†.

## 7. Kinds of events

Over the year, its records show these kinds of events:

- **Direct customers:** subscription renewals on a set day, failed renewals retried, cancellations and pauses, a price increase, promotion spikes, refunds, returns, chargebacks with fees.
- **Wholesale and institutional:** purchase orders and invoices on net-30 and net-60, late payment, a short payment over damage, an invoice held over paperwork, a large receivable at year end, a payment that lands after year end.
- **Financing:** a Shopify Capital advance repaid from payouts, revenue-share advances from two other lenders, fixed weekly debits on a line, several lenders taking from the same sales, a small monthly bank loan, all advances paid off by year end.
- **Purchases:** a deposit and balance wired to a manufacturer abroad, freight and duties when containers land, packaging, shipping labels and later adjustments.
- **People:** contractors on retainers and by project, a part-timer paid every two weeks, the founders paying themselves little.
- **Institutions:** daily payouts net of fees and holdbacks, wire fees, Brex autopay, monthly statements.
