# Shortlight: a short-drama streaming app paid through two app stores, web checkout and a phone carrier, across four regions and three companies (first quarter of 2025)

This describes one business, its past and one quarter. Facts marked † come from the real company's annual reports (Form 20-F and an earlier 10-K) that this business is modeled on (see `SOURCES.md`). Facts marked ‡ come from the app stores' published payout rules. A mark vouches for the fact it follows, not for the date or reason the story gives it. The companies, people and account numbers are invented.

This world is built for testing order-to-cash agents on money that arrives late, in lumps, net of fees and in many currencies. One viewer's `$19.99` membership is one line among hundreds of thousands inside a monthly app-store payment that covers four or five weeks, more than a hundred storefronts, refunds, taxes and a currency conversion. That payment lands in a Singapore company's bank account, its books are kept in Singapore dollars, and its parent reports in US dollars from another country.

## 1. The business and the time frame

- **Business:** Shortlight Pte. Ltd., based in Singapore†, runs Shortlight, a streaming app for short dramas: vertical episodes of one to two minutes that the company produces and translates itself and releases to a global audience†. Viewers are mostly in Europe, the Americas and Southeast Asia†. Shows are filmed in the United States, Mexico, Australia, Thailand and the Philippines†.
- **How it earns:**
  - **Coins.** Viewers buy coins in the app and spend them to unlock episodes. Coins cannot be transferred and can only be used in the app†. Revenue is recognized when the coins are spent†.
  - **Memberships.** Weekly, monthly and annual memberships unlock everything†. Revenue is recognized evenly over the membership period†.
  - **Advertising** shown in the app†.
  - **Licensing** its shows to other platforms, invoiced to a few large licensees†.
- **Size (the real company's year):** In 2025, memberships and coins brought in `$21,620,200`, advertising `$2,616,500` and licensing `$1,868,900`, a total of `$26,105,600`†, down from `$36,184,000` in 2024†. In-app revenue by region in 2025 was `$6,522,600` from the US and Canada, `$8,917,800` from Asia-Pacific, `$5,022,500` from Europe, the Middle East and Africa, and `$1,157,300` from Latin America†. Fees charged by app stores and payment processors were `$5,947,000`† and amortization of the shows `$4,995,900`†. Heavy spending on Facebook and TikTok ads to win viewers† left a net loss of `$34,296,300`†.
- **This quarter (invented, consistent with the year):** viewers pay about `$5.8 million` through all channels, in about 480,000 paid transactions, a little over 5,000 a day. About 55% comes through Apple, 32% through Google, 11% through web checkout and 2% through the carrier deal that starts in January. Coin packs run from `$4.99` to `$99.99`. A weekly membership is `$19.99`, monthly `$39.99`, annual `$199.99`, with local prices in each storefront.
- **Time range:** 2025-01-01 to 2025-03-31, 90 days. The parent reports in USD. The Singapore company keeps its books in SGD. The quarter includes:
  - Apple's fiscal months, which end on Saturdays: February 1, March 1 and March 29 (section 3).
  - Google's monthly payments around January 15, February 15 and March 15.
  - New Year's Day, Martin Luther King Jr. Day (January 20) and Presidents' Day (February 17) for US banks.
  - Chinese New Year (January 29-30) for Singapore banks.
  - A quarter end on Monday, March 31, which is a public holiday in Singapore (Hari Raya Puasa).

## 2. History before the start

- **The parent's past.** The parent is a company that has changed what it does several times. It once leased aircraft through a California management company that was dissolved in December 2023†. It later ran an NFT game business† and earned a little from crypto staking†. In January 2024 it bought the company that runs the app†, which brought about `$1.7 million` of unearned viewer money (contract liabilities) onto its books†. In 2024 it moved its legal home to the Cayman Islands. The old Delaware company became its wholly owned subsidiary†. The Delaware company owns a British Virgin Islands holding company, and that holding company owns Shortlight Pte. Ltd.†
- **The three companies that move money in this world:**
  - **Shortlight Holdings Inc. (Cayman), the listed parent.** It raises money and reports in USD. In this world it has no bank account of its own and acts through the Delaware company.
  - **Shortlight US Corp. (Delaware).** Holds the group's US dollars. Pays US film crews, US lawyers and US auditors' invoices, and sends money to Singapore.
  - **Shortlight Pte. Ltd. (Singapore).** Owns the app and the shows, collects all viewer and licensing money, pays the Asian crews, translators and the Singapore staff.
- **The business email compromise.** In December 2021 the Delaware company fell for a business email compromise scam†. Since then it has required extra confirmation before any large money transfer†. In this world that means a phone call back to a number already on file before any wire over `$25,000`.
- **Controls.** The group's controls over financial reporting were not effective. The reason is a lack of enough financial reporting and accounting staff†. The finance team is three people in Singapore and one part-time bookkeeper in the US.
- **On 2025-01-01:**
  - Apple's December fiscal month (December 1 to December 28, 2024) is earned but not paid. Its payment arrives in late January.
  - Google's December earnings are due around January 15.
  - Stripe holds a few days of web sales. PayPal holds balances in USD, EUR, GBP and AUD that nobody has converted since November.
  - About `$1.1 million` of coins have been sold but not spent, and about `$0.6 million` of memberships are paid but not yet earned.
  - Ad networks owe October to December earnings.
  - Three licensing invoices are open. Two licensees hold most of the receivables, as at the end of 2024, when two customers held 38.3% and 15.7%†.
  - Singapore owes the Delaware company about `$9.4 million` of past funding, recorded by each side as an intercompany balance.
  - December invoices from a Los Angeles production company and two location owners are unpaid.

## 3. Institutions (the only sources of data)

Each keeps its own records the way it really would.

- **Apple (App Store Connect).**
  - **Sales reports.** A daily sales report lists every purchase, renewal and refund by storefront, in the customer's currency, with the customer price and the developer's proceeds.
  - **Fiscal months.** Apple's financial reports and payments follow Apple's fiscal calendar, not calendar months‡. Its months are four or five weeks long, Sunday to Saturday. In this quarter the fiscal months are December 29 to February 1, February 2 to March 1 and March 2 to March 29.
  - **Monthly financial report.** At the end of each fiscal month there is one report per region: units, proceeds in the local currency, taxes withheld, adjustments for refunds, the exchange rate used and the amount in USD.
  - **Payment.** Apple pays within 45 days of the last day of the fiscal month‡, usually about 33 days after. It pays into the account and currency on file‡. Apple's bank combines all regions into a single USD payment for the month‡. A region whose earnings are below its minimum threshold is held and carried into a later month‡.
  - **Commission.** Apple keeps 30% of a sale, and 15% of a subscription renewal once the subscriber has paid for more than a year. Proceeds are already net of the sales taxes and VAT that Apple collects and pays in many countries.
  - **Refunds.** Apple decides refunds itself. Each one shows up as a negative line in a later report.
- **Google (Play Console and payments profile).**
  - **Reports.** An earnings report lists every order, refund and chargeback in the buyer's currency, with Google's service fee, tax, the exchange rate and the amount in USD.
  - **Payment.** Orders processed in a calendar month are paid around the 15th of the next month‡. That month's refunds and chargebacks are netted off‡.
  - **Fees.** Google's fee follows the same pattern as Apple's.
- **Web checkout on the Shortlight website.** Viewers who buy coins or memberships on the web pay through two third-party processors, whose fees appear alongside the app stores' fees in the platform fees line†.
  - **Stripe.** Cards in local currencies. Pays out daily in USD to the Singapore company's USD account, converting other currencies for a fee. Disputes and dispute fees come out of the balance.
  - **PayPal.** Keeps a separate balance for each currency until someone converts it and withdraws it. Buyer claims and chargebacks are taken from the balance in the currency of the sale.
- **Telkomsel (an Indonesian mobile carrier).** Under an agreement signed on January 9, 2025†, Shortlight is offered to Indonesian phone users in data bundles in Telkomsel's own app†. In this world the payment terms are as follows (invented):
  - Telkomsel bills its own customers.
  - About 30 days after each month ends, it sends a statement in Indonesian rupiah with active users and Shortlight's share.
  - Once both sides agree the statement, it pays that share in USD, at its own month-end rate, 60 days after the month ends.
- **Two ad mediation networks.** Monthly statements of ad earnings in USD, paid 45 and 60 days after the month ends.
- **Licensees.** Three streaming platforms in Asia and Latin America license shows for a flat fee per season, invoiced in USD on signing and on delivery. One of them must withhold tax on payments to foreign companies.
- **Straits Harbour Bank (Singapore).** Shortlight Pte. Ltd. has a USD account `••7720` and an SGD account `••7704`.
  - **USD account.** Apple, Google, Stripe, licensees, ad networks and the Delaware company pay into it.
  - **SGD account.** Pays Singapore payroll and local suppliers.
  - **Conversions.** Converting between the two accounts happens at the bank's rate on the day. Every incoming telegraphic transfer shows the sender's name as their bank sent it, and sometimes a bank charge is deducted along the way.
- **Coastline Commerce Bank (US).** Shortlight US Corp.'s operating account `••3391`. Holds the money the parent raised, wires money to Singapore, and pays US vendors by ACH and wire.
- **The books.**
  - **Singapore company.** Uses Xero in SGD. USD receipts are translated at the day's rate.
  - **Delaware company.** Uses QuickBooks Online in USD.
  - **Consolidation.** The CFO combines the companies in a spreadsheet each quarter, removing intercompany balances.
- **The app's own database.** Every purchase with its channel, storefront, currency and price, each user's coin balance (bought coins and free bonus coins kept apart), every coin spent and on which episode, and every membership with its start and end dates. Its order ids match the stores' transaction ids. It does not know about fees, taxes or exchange rates.
- **Payroll.** Singapore payroll each month, with employer contributions to the national pension fund. US crews are paid by invoice through the Delaware company.

## 4. The cast

- **Mei Lin Tan, CFO, Singapore.** Signs off the quarter close and the consolidation. Approves every wire over `$25,000` after the call-back. Under pressure to file on time with too few people.
- **Arjun Pillai, finance manager, Singapore.** Matches each app-store payment to the reports behind it and to the app's database. Keeps the coin and membership liability schedule. Careful but always a month behind.
- **Grace Ong, accountant, Singapore.** Joins on February 3. Handles payables, intercompany invoices and bank feeds. New to app-store accounting.
- **Paul Hendry, part-time bookkeeper, US.** Keeps the Delaware company's books two days a week. Books every wire to Singapore the way the email describing it says.
- **Daniel Reyes, head of US production, Los Angeles.** Hires crews and locations for the US shows. Sends invoices to the Delaware company, often after the shoot.
- **Hannah Wu, growth lead.** Runs ads on Facebook and TikTok, paid by card and by invoice. Runs coin promotions with extra free coins.
- **Viewers (millions of installs, a few hundred thousand paying in the quarter).** Buy coin packs at night, binge, and ask Apple for refunds. Forget weekly memberships and dispute them on the web. Some use stolen cards on the web checkout.
- **Licensees.** Three platforms. One pays on time, one pays late and in pieces, and one withholds tax and sends the certificate weeks later.
- **Telkomsel's partner manager.** Friendly, slow on paperwork, counts active users differently from the app.

## 5. Money flows

| Flow | How it moves |
|---|---|
| Viewers -> Apple -> Singapore USD account | one USD payment per fiscal month, about 33 days after it ends‡, net of commission, taxes and refunds, with low-earning regions held back‡ |
| Viewers -> Google -> Singapore USD account | one payment around the 15th for the previous calendar month‡ |
| Viewers -> Stripe -> Singapore USD account | daily payouts, converted to USD for a fee, less disputes |
| Viewers -> PayPal balances (USD, EUR, GBP, AUD) -> Singapore USD account | converted and withdrawn by hand, about every two weeks |
| Indonesian phone users -> Telkomsel -> Singapore USD account | statement in rupiah 30 days after month end; USD payment 60 days after month end, once agreed |
| Ad networks -> Singapore USD account | monthly, 45 or 60 days after month end |
| Licensees -> Singapore USD account | wire against invoices, sometimes short by withholding tax or bank charges |
| Delaware company -> Singapore USD account | intercompany funding wires, a few million dollars at a time |
| Delaware company -> US crews, locations, lawyers, auditors | ACH and wire against invoices |
| Singapore USD account -> SGD account | conversions at the bank's rate to fund payroll and local bills |
| Singapore accounts -> Asian crews, translators, staff, ad platforms | bank transfers, card payments, monthly payroll |

## 6. Stories

- **The lump in January.** On about January 30, Apple's payment for the fiscal month that ended December 28 lands as one USD wire, with only a short reference. It covers four weeks of December sales in more than a hundred storefronts, net of commission, VAT and refunds, converted at Apple's rates. Three small regions in Africa and Central Asia are held back below their thresholds and carried into a later month. Arjun has to split it by region and currency, match it to the app's December, which runs on calendar months, and explain why the two never agree to the cent.
- **Chinese New Year coins (January 24-February 2).** Hannah runs a promotion that adds 30% free coins to every pack. Sales in Asia-Pacific double for a week. The free coins carry no revenue, so when viewers spend them, the revenue on the coins they bought has to be spread over both. The app's database keeps the two kinds of coins apart. The spreadsheet that Arjun uses does not.
- **The carrier deal (January-March).** The Telkomsel agreement is signed on January 9† and goes live on January 20. January's statement, in rupiah, arrives on March 4. It counts fewer active users than the app does, because Telkomsel counts only users who watched at least once in the month. The two sides agree a number on March 21, and the first payment falls due in April, so at quarter end it is a receivable with a disputed history.
- **Withholding tax (February).** A licensee pays a `$180,000` delivery invoice as `$162,000`, keeping 10% as tax withheld for its government. The withholding certificate, which lets Shortlight claim the tax back, arrives on March 26. Until then, the receivable looks short-paid.
- **The licensee who paid a stranger (March).** A licensee's finance team receives an email that looks like it comes from Shortlight, from a look-alike domain, with a letter on Shortlight's letterhead giving a "new bank account". The licensee pays a `$240,000` invoice there. Two weeks later, when Arjun chases it, the licensee sends proof of payment. Shortlight's receivable is still open, the licensee believes it has paid, and the two companies argue over who bears the loss while the licensee's bank tries to recall the wire.
- **Funding from the parent.** The Delaware company wires `$2,000,000` to Singapore on January 14 and `$1,500,000` on March 12. Paul books both as loans to Singapore, as the emails describing them say. Grace books the March wire as a capital contribution, because Mei Lin's board memo calls it one. At quarter end, the intercompany balances don't eliminate. Part of the gap is that wrong booking, and part is exchange rates, because Singapore's books are in SGD and the Delaware company's are in USD.
- **The Los Angeles shoot (February).** Daniel's crew shoots two series in Los Angeles for the Singapore company. The Delaware company pays `$410,000` of crew, location and equipment invoices. It sends Singapore a recharge invoice on March 28, so the shows' costs sit in the wrong company for most of the quarter.
- **Price tier mistake (February).** For six days, a monthly membership in four Latin American storefronts is set to the wrong price tier, about a tenth of the intended price. Hundreds of viewers buy it. When it is fixed, the app shortens their memberships instead of charging more. Apple's February report shows the low proceeds. The app's database first shows the intended price and is corrected later.
- **Weekly membership disputes (all quarter).** Viewers on the web checkout who forgot their weekly membership dispute three or four renewals at once. Stripe takes each disputed amount and a fee from the balance. Some disputes are won with viewing logs, and most are lost. PayPal claims come out of the EUR and GBP balances.
- **PayPal balances (January and March).** Arjun converts the EUR, GBP and AUD balances only twice in the quarter. Each conversion happens at PayPal's rate, which includes a spread, and leaves a little in each currency.
- **The quarter close (March 29-April).** Mei Lin has to close a quarter whose last two days (March 30-31) fall in Apple's next fiscal month, with Apple's February and March fiscal months earned but not yet paid, Google's March unpaid, two months of ad earnings unpaid and the Telkomsel receivable just agreed. She needs the coin and membership liability at March 31, the receivables from each platform, the intercompany balances eliminated and the SGD books translated to USD. March 31 is a bank holiday in Singapore, so the bank statement's last posting date is March 28.

## 7. Kinds of events

Over the quarter, its records show these kinds of events:

- **App-store collections:** monthly payments covering fiscal months that differ from calendar months, regions held below threshold and released later, refunds netted from later payments, commission at two rates, taxes withheld at the source, exchange rates chosen by the store.
- **Web collections:** daily payouts with currency conversion fees, disputes and dispute fees, won and lost disputes, balances held in several currencies, manual conversions with a spread.
- **Carrier billing:** a new partner, a statement in a foreign currency, a disagreement over usage, a payment after quarter end.
- **Business-to-business receivables:** licensing invoices, late and partial payments, withholding tax with a certificate later, bank charges deducted along the way, a customer that paid a fraudster's account.
- **Unearned revenue:** coins sold, coins spent, free bonus coins, memberships earned evenly over their period, refunds of partly used memberships, a price mistake.
- **Several companies and currencies:** intercompany funding wires, a recharge for costs paid on another company's behalf, intercompany balances that don't agree, books in SGD translated into USD, conversions between the company's own accounts.
- **Period end:** money earned but not yet paid by each platform, a quarter end on a holiday, and app-store months that stop two days before the quarter ends.

## 8. What the world must also remember

So that an agent working these records can be graded, the world keeps, in its own state and not in any institution's records, the truth behind every record:

- which viewer purchases, refunds, taxes, fees and exchange rates make up each app-store, processor and carrier payment, and which regions are being held back;
- the coins sold, coins spent and bonus coins at every day's end, with the correct unearned coin and membership balances at March 31;
- the correct receivable from each platform at March 31;
- which licensing invoices each payment settles, and the withholding tax still to be reclaimed;
- that the licensee's payment went to a fraudster, and the amount, if any, recovered;
- the right booking for each intercompany wire and recharge, and the part of the intercompany difference that comes from exchange rates rather than from errors.
